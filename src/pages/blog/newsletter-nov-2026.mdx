The new DPI Map is live. Over Q2 and Q3 we reviewed our full methodology, re-examined multiple countries across digital ID, payments and data exchange, and rebuilt how we record and publish what we find. In total we made 2,200+ edits reflected across the website. 

We are very excited about this new work. The main headline for you isn’t that there is more data (there is!). It's that we are giving researchers more powerful ways to check that data. Every indicator now carries its own history of changes, with the date, our rationale, and the sources behind it. We now distinguish between what we looked for and couldn't find and what we haven't yet researched. And where we got something wrong, we say so and show the fix. 

All of this sits on a new website that is now the single source of truth for the Map, and which is, frankly, a pleasure to use. Each country page opens with how the country measures up against our DPI-likeness criteria, and allows users to easily click through to neighboring countries. The website also features a revamped Insights page that brings together all our team’s writing on DPI.

![Denmark's country page on the new DPI Map: its digital ID, digital payment and data exchange systems side by side, each with its DPI test and fields](/blog/newsletter-nov-2026/denmark-country-page.png)

This issue sets out how we now build the Map, what this round of research found, and what's new on the site. 

## How we build the Map now

A dataset is only useful if people can trust it, check it, and build on it. This release changes how we do all three.

### Every change has a paper trail

Every indicator now shows its full history: what changed, when, why, and the sources and links behind each decision. If a country's status moves, you can see what moved it. If you disagree with one of our calls, you can see the reasoning you're disagreeing with. You can also trace each codebook variable back to the broader methodology framework, for example, to see which measures feed into our assessment of interoperability.

![A field's history on a system page: TIPS's status of implementation changing from Implemented to Planned/Piloted, with the date, the reason and the source](/blog/newsletter-nov-2026/field-history.png)

### Telling evidence gaps from research gaps

Every variable now has a "Not found" option. "Not found" means we looked but couldn't find enough information to make a definitive call, including where a system isn't live yet. "NA" means we haven't researched it. Previously both these categories were covered by NA. Since we’re now using distinct tags, you can easily see whether the evidence is missing or our work is.

### We correct our mistakes in public

We aren't always right, and when we aren't, we say so.

In previous releases, TIPS, the Eurosystem's instant payment settlement service, was wrongly coded to several European countries that do not use it. This round we removed seven incorrect TIPS entries from the United Kingdom, Czech Republic, Romania, Poland, Hungary, Ukraine and Liechtenstein, and replaced each with the country’s own instant payment system. We also recoded TIPS as planned rather than live for Norway and Iceland, which are due to join from 2028. Three of the four decreases in the payments table come from these corrections: the United Kingdom, because Faster Payments is industry-run rather than central bank-run, and Norway and Iceland, because TIPS is not yet live there. The other six replacements left those countries’ counts unchanged. 

Our bar for deleting a system is high, but when the evidence shows we got it wrong, we fix it and show our work.

### Tracking setbacks, not just progress

Implementation status now includes a "Suspended" category. Projects get deprioritised, paused or shelved, and a map that only records progress tells half the story. Our first recorded suspension is DCash 2.0, the Eastern Caribbean Central Bank’s retail CBDC platform. In 2026, the Eastern Caribbean Central Bank’s Monetary Council [suspended development](https://discovermni.com/2026/02/16/eccb-suspends-dcash-2-0-to-prioritise-regional-fast-payment-system/) to prioritise work on a fast payment system and participation in the CARICOM Payment and Settlement System pilot. 

### Built to be cited and maintained

Each system now has a persistent unique identifier, linked to its country or regional code. That keeps records stable over time and makes them easier to cite and to update as systems launch, merge or close. Countries follow the ISO 3166 standard, with a few exceptions that are clearly noted. Regions now follow the UN M49 standard: Africa, Asia, Europe and Oceania, with the Americas split into the M49 sub-regions of Northern America and Latin America and the Caribbean. 

The website is now the single source of truth for the Map: every other DPI Map asset derives from it, so there is one canonical version of the data.

Definitions, categories and coding rules are set out in the upgraded codebook at [dpimap.org/codebook](https://dpimap.org/codebook/).

## What this round found

This round covered 31 countries in digital ID, 38 countries in payments and 50 countries in data exchange.

### <TypeIcon dpiType="id" class="inline size-5 -mt-1 mr-1.5" />Digital ID

| Countries reviewed | Added to DPI-like count | Removed from DPI-like count | Net change in DPI-like count |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 31 | +10 | 0 | +10 |

Ten systems joined the DPI-like count. Cyprus, Djibouti, Honduras, Hungary, Jamaica, Kenya and Laos moved to Implemented, and there were new digital ID developments in Belize, Burundi and Cuba. 

New information was uncovered. For example, we found evidence of multi-sector use cases in Andorra, Armenia, Bahrain, Cyprus and Djibouti. 

Digital ID has moved more slowly than payments and data exchange since the Map launched. Much of this round's movement came from systems reaching implementation, alongside rebrands and new interfaces. To learn more, [read our ID post](/blog/digital-id-whats-new-nov-2026/). 

### <TypeIcon dpiType="pay" class="inline size-5 -mt-1 mr-1.5" /> Payments

| Countries reviewed | Added to DPI-like count | Removed from DPI-like count | Net change in DPI-like count |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 38  | +12 | -4 | +8 |

Twelve countries joined the DPI-like count. PI-SPI is now live in eight countries, Barbados has implemented BIMPay, and Indonesia's BI-FAST joins the count. We also have new records for Vietnam (NAPAS) and Laos (LAPNet). Four systems left the count: Three because of the TIPS correction described above, and Peru’s CBDC system being downgraded from implemented to pilot. 

In total we added 11 new systems to the dataset, six of them replacing the incorrect TIPS entries. We also replaced the single "settlement system" field with two: clearing infrastructure and settlement method.

Across the 38 countries we looked at, most appear to have access to some form of real-time payments architecture. In some cases, central banks are adding real-time functionality to existing infrastructure. Where regional blocs share a currency, they're building common infrastructure, and TIPS and now PI-SPI are the clearest examples. We'll be watching how this evolves. To learn more about the settlement change and what we observed, [read our payments post](/blog/payments-whats-new-nov-2026/). 

### <TypeIcon dpiType="data" class="inline size-5 -mt-1 mr-1.5" /> Data exchange

| Countries reviewed | Countries added to DPI-like count | Countries removed from DPI-like count | Net change in DPI-like count |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 50 | 0 | 2 | -2 |

In Data Exchanges, a better conceptualisation of DPI-like data exchange systems was the broader focus of our work this quarter which resulted in the replacement of several systems as well. In Germany and Ireland we replaced our previous entries - [Federal Information Management](https://interoperable-europe.ec.europa.eu/sites/default/files/inline-files/NIFO_2024%20Supporting%20Document_Germany_vFinal_0.pdf) and [Build to Share](https://www.ogcio.gov.ie/en/corporate-pages/policy/build-to-share/) - with two national data exchanges in pilot stages. This shifted their status from DPI-like to Work-In-Progress (WIP).

However, we also added DPI-like systems to two countries  — namely Belgium’s CrossRoad’s Bank for Social Security which allows for the exchange of social security linked data for cross-sectoral use and Greece’s Interoperability Centre which is legally recognised as the sole data exchange platform between public sector entities. This does not add to our overall country count but presents new information about multiple DPI-like exchanges in countries for the first time. 

Overall, we added 13 other systems to the dataset that replaced other data exchanges that we had incorrectly tracked on the Map. 

All of them replace earlier entries that turned out to be document-management platforms, government networks, catalogues or frameworks rather than data exchange infrastructures. Further, as part of our ongoing work on reviewing our data exchange systems measurement, we also added six new fields: formal mandate, once-only principle, personal data protection, independent audit, number of transactions and number of members. And we replaced the yes/no semantic interoperability field with one that distinguishes semantic from syntactic interoperability.

To learn more about the new fields and what we observed, [read our data exchange post](/blog/data-exchange-whats-new-nov-2026/).
